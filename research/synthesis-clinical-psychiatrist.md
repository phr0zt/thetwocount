# The Death of the Ego: A Clinical Reading of Jackson, Friston, and Dalio

*A synthesis written from twenty years treating patients through trauma, dissociation, depersonalization, and psychedelic-assisted therapy.*

---

## The frame I'm applying

None of these three men is talking about the same phenomenon, even though they're using the same vocabulary. Jackson is talking about a *relational* surrender — subordinating self-interest to a team, inside a bounded, seasonal, embodied relationship with an authority figure. Friston is describing a *computational* event — precision-weighting turned down on a hierarchy of priors, a value-neutral redescription of what happens in any system, healthy or sick, that stops trusting its own model of itself. Dalio is describing an *institutional* practice — continuous, structural exposure designed to make ego-defense economically costly to maintain.

Clinically, I care about exactly one variable that all three of them underweight: **who holds the person while the self-model is destabilized, and can the person say no.** Ego dissolution is not, in my experience, good or bad in itself — dissociation is dissolution too, and so is a bad manic episode, and so is falling in love, and so is six years of analysis. What determines whether a given instance of "losing yourself" is generative or damaging is almost never the depth of the dissolution. It's the *scaffolding* around it: consent, dosing, a present attuned other, and a path back. Judge Jackson, Friston, and Dalio against that scaffold, and their convergences and failures become much sharper than "ego bad, selflessness good" lets on.

---

## Where they converge — and why the convergence is clinically real

All three, independently, arrive at the same structural insight that any decent trauma therapist or DBT clinician would recognize immediately: **you cannot argue a defense mechanism out of existence.** You cannot tell someone "stop being defensive" and have it work, because the demand itself gets processed by the very system you're trying to bypass. So all three build *environments* instead of making *arguments*.

- Jackson builds it into the geometry of the offense (the triangle has no primary ball-handler; the two-count rule makes holding the ball structurally impossible, not merely discouraged).
- Friston's framework — even though descriptive rather than prescriptive — explains *why* direct confrontation fails: high-precision priors are, by definition, priors the system will defend against contradicting evidence. You don't out-argue a high-precision belief; you have to change the gain on the whole hierarchy.
- Dalio builds it into infrastructure (the Dot Collector, radical transparency, mandatory public disagreement) precisely because he doesn't trust "unaided individual humility to survive contact with ego," in the words of the mental-models analysis.

This is a genuinely correct clinical intuition, and it's worth naming plainly: **structure beats insight-based persuasion for changing defended self-states.** It's why exposure therapy works better than telling a phobic patient their fear is irrational, why 12-step programs use ritual and structure rather than willpower appeals, and why psychedelic-assisted therapy insists on *set and setting* rather than just handing someone a compound and a pep talk. All three men have rediscovered, from basketball, physics, and finance respectively, something psychiatry already knows about behavior change. That's a legitimate point in favor of all three frameworks, and I don't want what follows to read as dismissing it.

Where they converge a second time, more troublingly: **none of them treats the destabilizing event as requiring an endpoint.** All three are far more articulate about how to take a self-model apart than about what happens after. That absence is where I have real arguments with each of them, and it's where the harm shows up.

## Case 1: Carmelo Anthony — what a clinician sees that a sports journalist doesn't

The journalistic framing of the Melo/Jackson dispute is "star player couldn't accept coaching" versus "coach failed to connect with a star." Both readings treat this as a dispute about basketball philosophy or personality clash. I read it as something more specific and more diagnosable: **a rupture in the therapeutic frame, in a relationship that was functioning, whether either man named it or not, as a quasi-therapeutic one.**

Jackson's own account of his method — meditation, ritual (the line-crossing rite, sage, dimmed lights), asking players to "check that attitude at the door," modeling vulnerability through self-disclosure, deliberately using indirection to avoid triggering ego-defense — is not a coaching philosophy in the ordinary sense. It's a set of techniques borrowed wholesale from contemplative and group-therapeutic practice, applied to a population (elite young men, often with attachment histories shaped by early fame and "entourage" dependency, as Jackson himself notes) for whom that kind of intervention carries real psychological weight. Once you're doing candlelit sessions, asking someone to surrender the self-protective posture that got them this far, and orchestrating meetings meant to "shift someone's attitude" through modeling — you have taken on a duty of care that basketball coaching, formally, does not require. Carmelo's own language confirms he experienced it that way: "we had zen moments." That's not the language of a player describing a tactical adjustment. That's the language of someone describing a relationship in which something real and intimate was offered.

Which is exactly why his complaint lands as clinically serious rather than merely sour grapes: *"Instead of coming down there and coaching, you sat in the stands."* In attachment terms, this is the textbook complaint against an insufficiently available caregiver who nonetheless prescribes vulnerability — ask someone to open the door, and then not be behind it. Every modality that deliberately destabilizes a person's defenses — trauma-focused therapy, psychedelic-assisted therapy, intensive group work — has a non-negotiable structural requirement: a present, responsive, accountable other during the vulnerable window. Psychedelic therapy protocols require a trained sitter in the room for exactly this reason; absence during the destabilized phase is a known predictor of adverse outcomes, not an incidental detail. Jackson's own doctrine even names the mechanism that makes his absence so costly: he writes elsewhere that his own ego-dialing-back — staying in the background, distributing power — is the *precondition* that makes players' surrender safe. Carmelo is arguing, in effect, that the precondition didn't hold for him: that what Jackson calls "dialing back ego" collapsed, in his specific case, into ordinary disengagement, and that the two are indistinguishable from the position of someone who has already lowered his guard.

The detail that should trouble anyone thinking clinically here is the "showed him film of Jordan doing it right" intervention. Analogical teaching — showing an exemplar rather than commanding — is a legitimate technique when there's enough rapport and safety for the recipient to self-persuade rather than feel indicted. But used with an already-struggling player, "here's the guy who did this correctly, unlike you" is not modeling. It's a comparison that manufactures shame, delivered in the guise of gentle indirection. Shame-based correction dressed as Zen non-confrontation is still shame-based correction — arguably worse, because the wrapping makes it harder for the recipient to name what happened, or to be believed when he tries to. Carmelo naming it publicly, years later, on his own podcast, is the clinical signature of someone who has finally gotten enough distance to identify an injury that didn't have a name at the time.

None of this means Jackson is a villain or that "ego surrender" as a concept is fraudulent. It means: **Jackson's system has no internal mechanism for detecting its own failure of presence**, because withdrawal and strategic restraint are designed to look identical from the inside. That's not a moral flaw in Jackson personally — it's a structural blind spot in any framework that relies on the authority figure's own non-attachment as the load-bearing safety mechanism, with no external check on whether that non-attachment has curdled into abandonment. A hospital has an ethics committee. A therapy practice has supervision and a licensing board. Jackson had "no mind."

## Case 2: Friston's precision-weighting — mechanistically elegant, clinically incomplete, and dangerous exactly where it's most quotable

I want to be fair to Friston before I argue with him: the free energy framework, and especially the Laukkonen/Chandaria "Beautiful Loop" work, gives me genuinely useful clinical language. Patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder frequently describe something close to Friston's account with unnerving precision — a felt collapse of the "self" as a stable presence, while cognition, perception, and even insight remain intact ("I know I exist, I just can't feel it"). Telling such a patient that this is a real, describable computational event — a change in the confidence-weighting the brain assigns its own self-model — rather than a moral failure, an act of avoidance, or "just anxiety," is de-stigmatizing and often clinically useful on its own. Friston's "attractor" language (a bounded region of viable states, not a fixed essence) is also a better ontology of selfhood than either "you have a soul" or "the self is an illusion" — it gives patients something to hold onto that isn't binary.

But here is my actual argument with him, and it's not a quibble — it's the central limitation of a purely mechanistic account applied to a domain that is inherently evaluative.

**Precision-weighting is symmetric. Harm is not.** Depersonalization disorder and a "successful" psychedelic ego-dissolution experience are, in Friston's own vocabulary, the *same event*: a collapse in the temporal depth and precision of the self-model. One of these is a disabling illness that brings people to my office asking to feel real again; the other is being sold, in the culture this book is examining, as a spiritual technology to be sought out and repeated. The free energy principle, by its own design — its authors are explicit about this — cannot distinguish the two from the inside, because both are just settings of the same dial. What supplies the distinction is not more mathematics. It's clinical judgment: context, consent, trajectory, whether the person can still function, whether the state resolves or entrenches, whether it was chosen or happened to them. Friston's framework needs an entire second axis — call it a harm/functioning axis — that his own papers never construct, because it isn't a physics question. It's a psychiatric one. Until that axis exists and travels with the theory wherever it's popularized, "ego dissolution via precision reduction" reads to a lay audience as a single arrow pointing toward liberation, when the same arrow, read backward, is the diagnostic criteria for a dissociative disorder.

**FIBUS is not a footnote — it's the whole ballgame.** The dossier treats "False Insights and Beliefs Under Psychedelics" as Friston and Laukkonen's own epistemic-humility caveat, almost an aside next to the more exciting REBUS story. Clinically, I'd put it at the center. Here is what FIBUS actually describes, stripped of the physics language: a person's capacity for reality-testing is suppressed at precisely the moment their conviction in a novel belief is spiking, and the belief that wins out is whatever "coherently reduces long-run uncertainty" for that system in that moment — which has nothing to do with whether the belief is true, useful, or safe. I have sat with patients who came out of unsupervised psychedelic experiences newly convinced they'd uncovered a repressed memory that wasn't real, that a family member was dangerous, that they'd been "chosen," that ordinary medication was now poison because they'd "seen through" the illusion of illness. Every one of those beliefs was held with more certainty afterward than any belief they'd held before — which is exactly what you'd predict from a precision surge on an unvetted prior during a window of reduced hierarchical constraint. Friston's math explains why this happens better than anything else in the literature. It offers almost nothing on how to prevent it, detect it in real time, or treat it afterward, because those are questions about *dosing, screening, and integration* — the machinery of a clinical setting, not a Bayesian generative model.

What Friston's framework needs from clinical practice, concretely: (1) a titration variable — control over the rate and degree of precision change, which is exactly what "set and setting," screening for psychotic vulnerability, and trained facilitation provide and an unsupervised experience does not; (2) an integration protocol — a structured process, after the state resolves, for testing the new beliefs against reality with support, rather than letting whatever won the "inferential competition" during the peak experience simply become the new operating self-model unexamined; (3) a premorbid-vulnerability screen, because the same mechanism that produces insight in a well-regulated nervous system can produce a first psychotic break in a vulnerable one — the math doesn't know the difference, and neither does an unsupervised guide reading Friston's papers for justification.

I'd go further than the dossier's own critics (Stegemann, Thompson) here, because their objections are philosophical — falsifiability, category errors, boundary-placement. Mine is clinical: **a value-neutral mechanism, popularized as spiritual or therapeutic guidance without its evaluative complement attached, is not neutral in its effects.** It reliably gets read as permission. "Ego dissolution is just precision-weighting, and more flexibility is generally good" is a sentence I expect to see, unattributed and stripped of the FIBUS caveat, on a retreat center's website within the year. That is the danger this framework carries into the world, and neither Friston nor his collaborators, to their partial credit on the humility point, seem to fully reckon with how their descriptive elegance will be used prescriptively downstream.

## Case 3: Bridgewater — a laboratory-grade chronic stress environment wearing the language of liberation

Strip Dalio's vocabulary away and describe Bridgewater by its actual protocol: employees are rated in real time, on dozens of personal attributes, by peers and superiors, on a device visible to the room, with the results algorithmically weighted to determine how much their opinion counts going forward — and this is not an occasional event but the ambient condition of every meeting, indefinitely, with formal prohibition on processing disagreement privately. If I described that protocol to you without the word "Bridgewater" attached, you would recognize it immediately: it is close to a textbook social-evaluative threat paradigm — the same structure researchers use in the lab (public evaluation, unpredictability, no escape) to reliably induce a cortisol stress response, because that combination is one of the most reliable stressors we know how to produce in a human being. Dalio didn't discover a wellness technology. He built, at industrial scale and as a condition of employment, an instrument that the stress-physiology literature already tells us is aversive by design — and then, crucially, called the aversiveness the *point*, on the theory that "pain plus reflection equals progress."

Here's where I'd argue hardest with him: **chronic, inescapable social threat does not reliably produce openness. It reliably produces hypervigilance, and hypervigilance is not the opposite of ego-defense — it's ego-defense's most efficient form.** A person under continuous evaluative surveillance doesn't stop protecting their self-image; they get better at protecting it in ways that are legible to the surveillance system. They learn to perform the *appearance* of radical candor — the words, the willingness to "fight for right" in the room — because that performance is now what's being scored. This is not a hypothetical; it's the standard finding wherever surveillance and evaluation are made continuous and inescapable: behavior optimizes for the metric, not for the underlying virtue the metric was meant to proxy. What Dalio is very likely selecting for and rewarding, at scale, is a talent for *appearing* undefended under observation — which is a genuinely different, and in some ways more armored, psychological adaptation than actually being undefended. Real ego-softening happens in conditions of safety, where the cost of being wrong is low enough that curiosity beats self-protection. Bridgewater's design guarantees the opposite: the cost of looking wrong, or unbelievable, is your professional standing, in front of the room, every day. You can train people to disclose under those conditions. You cannot train them to be safe while disclosing, because the system is built to make disclosure permanently, structurally risky. That's not radical transparency. That's compelled testimony.

The line I'd flag hardest from the dossier is the evolution the mental-models analysis itself surfaces: Dalio's language shifting, across the very year studied, from "suspend your ego" (personal, almost therapeutic) to "openness is a responsibility... you are obliged to... what you're not allowed to do is complain privately" (institutional, disciplinary). That shift is diagnostic on its own. Removing a person's private, low-stakes channel for processing disagreement — venting to a colleague before deciding whether an issue is worth raising formally — is removing a normal, adaptive emotion-regulation behavior, not eliminating an ego-defense. In trauma-informed practice we draw a hard line between *voluntary* disclosure, at a pace the discloser controls, which is generally therapeutic, and *mandated* disclosure under threat of professional consequence, which correlates with worse outcomes across the disclosure literature generally (forensic interviewing, mandatory reporting contexts, compelled confession). Dalio has built a system that mandates the second and calls it the first.

And the informed-consent problem here is the starkest of the three case studies, not the mildest. A patient in psychotherapy can leave; the clinician is bound by a licensing board, a duty of confidentiality, and an ethics code that exists specifically because the clinician holds more power in the relationship. A Bridgewater employee's "consent" to have their believability continuously rated by an algorithm in front of peers is manufactured by ordinary employment dependency — salary, career trajectory, reputation in a small industry — with no equivalent professional body holding Dalio, or the Dot Collector's design, accountable to anyone. This is precisely the configuration I distrust most in twenty years of practice: an intervention with real personality-level psychological force, delivered by an entity with total economic leverage over the recipient and zero external accountability for how that force is used. That the intervention is described in the language of liberation — meritocracy, transparency, openness — makes it more concerning to me, not less, because it means the people inside it have been given a story in which their own discomfort is evidence they're doing it right.

I want to be fair here too: it's plausible some genuinely self-select for and thrive in this environment, and believability-weighting is a real corrective to the specific pathology of opaque hierarchies where seniority silently overrides better arguments. That's a legitimate design problem to solve. But the dossier's own blind-spot analysis makes the diagnostic point for me better than I could: Dalio has never, in the material collected, turned his own systems-thinking on the system itself — no audit of who calibrated "believability" in the first place, no acknowledgment of survivorship bias in retelling 1982 and 2008 as proof the machine works, no counter-example of the Dot Collector producing a bad outcome. **An institution that has run a high-intensity psychological intervention on its population for decades and has never published an adverse event is not running an idea meritocracy. It's running an experiment with no IRB and no exit interview data that reaches the founder's own account of it.** That absence is itself the finding.

## What all three collectively miss

Stepping back from the individual cases, four gaps recur across all three frameworks, and I think this is the actual original contribution a clinical lens brings to this book — not "which of these three is right," but the shape of what none of them built.

**1. No theory of reintegration.** All three are fluent about the mechanics of taking a self-model apart — the triangle offense, precision down-weighting, the Dot Collector — and strikingly silent about what happens after, who holds the person while the self-model rebuilds, and what marks the process as finished. In trauma and psychedelic-assisted therapy, this is not a footnote; it is understood to be where most of the actual therapeutic effect, or harm, occurs. The peak destabilization is not the treatment. The peak destabilization is the *precondition* for the treatment, which is the slow, supported work of building a new, less defended self-model back up with someone present to catch the pieces that don't fit anymore. Jackson gestures at this (roles for bench players, his own self-disclosure) and then abandons it exactly where it mattered most, with Carmelo. Friston's mathematics has no variable for it at all — the models describe collapse, not repair. Dalio's system has no "after," because there's no discrete session to integrate; the pressure is ambient and permanent, which means the nervous system never gets the chance to consolidate anything.

**2. No center of gravity on consent.** The single variable that best predicts, in my clinical experience and in the broader trauma literature, whether a destabilizing experience becomes growth or damage is whether it was controllable and chosen. Jackson assumes consent is established by signing a contract to play basketball — which is not consent to a quasi-therapeutic relational intervention. Friston's mathematics is agnostic by construction to whether a precision change was self-initiated or inflicted; the equations don't have a term for "did you agree to this." Dalio inverts consent into duty outright — "openness is a responsibility... you are obliged" — which is the exact rhetorical move that turns a virtue into a demand backed by institutional power, which is the pattern I'm most wary of after two decades of watching what "shed your ego" does when someone else is holding the leverage.

**3. No screening for who this is contraindicated for.** A defended, rigid self-structure is not uniformly pathological. For a trauma survivor, a reliable, stable sense of self is often a hard-won achievement, not an obstacle to enlightenment — it's the thing that trauma took away and that years of work rebuilt. Blanket prescriptions to dissolve the ego — whether delivered by a coach, a compound, or a corporate culture — can be actively harmful for people with trauma histories, emerging psychotic vulnerability, or fragile identity structure, and those are not rare populations among people drawn to intense self-transcending experiences: elite athletes whose identity is already fused with performance, hedge-fund employees selected precisely for high drive and perfectionism, psychedelic-seekers frequently self-medicating an undiagnosed condition. None of the three frameworks screens for this. All three, in different registers, treat resistance to ego-loss as something to be overcome rather than as information that might, for a given person, be correct.

**4. "Ego" is doing five jobs at once, and none of them separate it out.** Across all six documents, "ego" slides between narcissistic self-protection, healthy self-boundary, cognitive bias, professional identity, and simple pride, often within the same paragraph. A psychiatrist has to keep these apart, because the intervention for each is different and sometimes opposite: the defensive flinch that stops someone hearing feedback should probably soften; the boundary that lets a person say no to an exploitative demand should not. "Kill the ego" as a unifying slogan — which is closer to Dalio's and the popularized version of Friston's framing than to Jackson's more bounded, individualized version (Rodman) — asks for the destruction of several different psychological structures at once, and doesn't notice that at least one of them is load-bearing.

## Where I actually side with each of them

Since interpretation requires taking positions, not just cataloguing gaps, here is where I'd defend each thinker, and against whom.

**I side with Jackson** on the core structural insight — build the environment, don't lecture the defense — over Dalio's cruder version of the same idea. Jackson's bounded-tolerance approach to Rodman ("as long as it stays within team confines") is, of the three, the closest thing to good clinical formulation: individualized, non-uniform, respecting that not all self-assertion is pathological. His instinct that the *leader's* ego has to go first is correct and important. My argument with him is entirely about follow-through, not premise — Carmelo is a failure of practice, not of theory.

**I side with Friston** against both the popularizers who'll strip his humility caveats off and against the enactivist critics who want to throw out the whole apparatus. The self-as-attractor language is genuinely clinically useful, de-stigmatizing vocabulary for dissociative patients, and I'd rather have it than not. My argument with him is that a mechanism this quotable, released into a culture already primed to romanticize self-loss, needs its evaluative complement built in at the source, not left as an aside that gets dropped in transmission.

**I side with Dalio** on the diagnosis — ego and blind spots really are the two biggest barriers to seeing yourself clearly, and "pain plus reflection equals progress" is not wrong, it's just an incomplete restatement of ordinary post-traumatic growth. His own 1982 story is a legitimate account of hard-won humility. My argument with him is that what worked as one man's voluntary, self-directed reckoning does not scale into a humane practice just because you can formalize it into an app and a rating scale — the elegance of the mechanism is not evidence of its safety, and he has never, by his own account, checked.

## A working standard, for what it's worth

If I had to hand the book one clinical instrument to score all three systems against, it would be four questions, in this order: **Was it voluntary? Was it dosed? Was there a present, accountable other? Was there a path back?**

Jackson scores well on dosing (indirection, bounded tolerance) and badly on presence, at least once, at the moment it mattered most. Friston's framework doesn't ask any of these four questions — which is fine for physics and dangerous for a self-help paperback. Dalio fails voluntariness and dosing outright, by design, and calls the failure the feature.

None of them built the thing psychiatry already knows you need before you take someone's sense of self apart: not a better argument for why they should let go, but a reason to trust that someone competent is going to be there when they do.
