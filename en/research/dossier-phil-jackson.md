## PART 1: RECENT MATERIAL (August 2025 – August 2026)

### 1. NPR "All Things Considered" interview — Nov 3, 2025
**Source:** NPR/Juana Summers interview with Phil Jackson and Sam Smith, tied to the NBA's 75th-anniversary book. Syndicated across NPR member stations (KUNM, TPR, SDPB, Ideastream, WUNC, WYPR, WYSO, Wyoming Public Media). https://www.npr.org/2025/11/03/nx-s1-5385232/phil-jackson-reflects-on-past-legends-and-future-potential-for-nbas-75th-birthday

- **Key claim:** Jackson frames ego-protection as an act of *service*, not indulgence — he deliberately withheld himself from Michael Jordan's orbit to protect Jordan's psychological space, rather than using proximity to a superstar to inflate his own coaching profile.
- **Quote:** "I kept away from him and let him have his private life because I think that was the most valuable thing for him to have."
- **Co-author Sam Smith's framing of Jordan:** Jordan's competitiveness is "a sickness. It's called competitiveness" — described as something that transcends skill, closer to compulsion than ambition, which Jackson had to channel rather than fight.
- **On the game's current state:** Jackson criticizes the modern NBA's individualism-adjacent style — the three-point-heavy, isolation-driven game — as "stagnant," and proposes literally enlarging the court by 10+ feet to restore ensemble, running basketball over one-on-one spectacle.
- **On his own legacy/ego:** Asked to sum up his career: "Wow. Unbelievable. Lucky." He does not claim credit in grandiose terms — attributes it to circumstance ("lucky") and calls the era "legendary" but explicitly says "that era, I think it's over," a notably non-attached, past-tense framing of his own greatest achievement.

### 2. *Masters of the Game: A Conversational History of the NBA in 75 Legendary Players* — book with Sam Smith, released Oct 28–Nov 2025 (Penguin Press)
**Sources:** ESPN (Oct 29, 2025) https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/46768636/phil-jackson-unfixable-carmelo-relationship-led-knicks-exit ; Bleacher Report; Fox News; Amazon/Goodreads/Penguin Random House listings; Civilian Reader review (Jan 31, 2026) https://civilianreader.com/2026/01/31/quick-review-masters-of-the-game-by-sam-smith-phil-jackson-penguin-press/ ; Dan Patrick Show video (Nov 5, 2025).

This is Jackson's most substantial recent public statement — a 75-player oral history built as a running conversation with veteran Bulls beat writer Sam Smith. Reviews describe it as "part basketball history, part philosophy session," with uneven depth per player but consistent throughlines on character and team chemistry.

**Concrete example — Carmelo Anthony / triangle offense (the clearest recent case study in ego-vs-system):**
- Jackson on the failed relationship: "Unfortunately my relationship with Carmelo is kind of busted, and if he's going to be here, it's probably best that I go." / "I don't want Carmelo back on the team; we've got to find a way to trade him."
- Jackson's stated intervention method: he brought Anthony into his office and **showed him film of Michael Jordan running the triangle offense**, using Jordan as a model of a superstar who subordinated individual scoring instinct to system reads.
- Jackson still credits Anthony's individual talent while framing the failure as a refusal to submit to structure: "Carmelo wants a championship; he wants to be on a team that has a chance, and he should be; he's a Hall of Famer" — but the triangle "depended on shared reads, structure, and patience," which Anthony's isolation-based game never accepted.

**The pushback — a rare live disagreement about Jackson's ego philosophy from someone who lived it:**
- Anthony, responding on his podcast "7PM in Brooklyn" (reported by Yahoo Sports, HoopsHype, Complex, SportsRush, BasketNews, CBS Sports, Fox News, ~late Oct/early Nov 2025): "We had zen moments" — recalling literal candlelit sessions in Jackson's office — but accused Jackson of hypocrisy: "Instead of bringing me to office, showing me Michael Jordan clips of the triangle and telling me what not to do in a triangle — because he did it wrong... You should have come down there and coached. Instead of sitting your a** up in the stand, come down here and coach. Coach the triangle." Anthony's counter-narrative: he "took the bullets" publicly for the triangle's failure while Jackson stayed detached (tweeting from the stands) rather than embodying the selfless, present coaching Jackson preaches. This is a genuine, recent, on-the-record disagreement over whether Jackson's ego-dissolution system requires the *coach's* ego-surrender too, not just the player's.

### 3. "Six Leadership Tips from Coach Phil Jackson" — University of Wisconsin School of Education, published Aug 8, 2025
**Source:** https://education.wisc.edu/news/six-leadership-tips-from-coach-phil-jackson/

Falls squarely in the 12-month window (Aug 2025). Direct pedagogical distillation of his ego-management coaching method:
- Tips: develop credibility; own your mistakes; **de-personalize feedback**; know when not to step in; create a shared team language; find meaningful roles for everyone (explicitly including low-minute players 9–12).
- Quote on de-personalizing correction (a core ego-dissolution mechanic — separating the person from the act so criticism doesn't trigger ego-defense): "It's not that I'm correcting you. I'm correcting the act that you need to change. I'm not demeaning you."
- Quote on voice/authority as a substitute for domination: "A lot of coaching depends on voice, on essence, on how you speak, the control that you have of the language, your ability to deliver a message."
- Quote on self-disclosure as leadership: "You make mistakes. And the mistakes you're going to make are sometimes how you endear yourself to your community" — i.e., the leader's own ego-transparency (admitting fallibility) is what earns the trust needed to ask players to surrender theirs.
- On bench players' egos: "If players 9-12 are unhappy with their roles, you want to find another way to get them involved. Keep them happy, content, and involved" — selflessness has to be built for the whole roster, not just enforced on stars.

### 4. All-Star Game criticism — Yahoo Sports, published Aug 16, 2025
**Source:** https://sports.yahoo.com/article/slap-face-basketball-phil-jackson-135800963.html

- Quote: "It's a slap to the face of basketball by making it a game of only superstars who only play one-on-one." Players "don't play a conceptualized game where five people play together."
- This is Jackson's clearest recent articulation of what he sees as basketball's *opposite* of his philosophy: unchecked individual ego on display as spectacle. His stated core purpose of coaching: "to get five people to play together." He also notes he felt structurally unsuited to coach Team USA/all-star rosters — his method requires sustained relational trust-building, not a temporary assembly of undissolved individual egos.

### 5. Horace Grant on Jackson's indirect ego-management technique — reported ~April 2026 (also covered by Newsweek)
**Source:** Yahoo Sports https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/horace-grant-phil-jacksons-workaround-183000927.html ; Newsweek https://www.newsweek.com/sports/nba/bulls-legend-reveals-wild-phil-jackson-coaching-strategy-11137637

- Grant's account of a specific in-game intervention technique: Jackson would publicly criticize Grant for turnovers Grant hadn't actually committed (Grant rarely handled the ball), knowing Jordan and Pippen would understand the message was really meant for them.
- Quote: "He wasn't really yelling at me…. he was yelling at Michael and Scottie. But, he would look at me, and [say], 'Horace, you got to stop turning the ball over.'"
- This is a concrete, named intervention: rather than directly confronting the fragile egos of his two best players (which risked triggering defensiveness and fracturing team cohesion), Jackson routed the correction through a lower-status, secure teammate — protecting the stars' egos from a face-threatening public callout while still landing the message. It's a mechanism for enforcing accountability *without* activating ego-defense.

## PART 2: HISTORICAL / BACKGROUND MATERIAL (clearly not from the last 12 months, but foundational to Jackson's ego philosophy)

### A. *Sacred Hoops: Spiritual Lessons of a Hardwood Warrior* (1995)
- Foundational statement of intent: "The day I took over the Chicago Bulls, I vowed to create an environment based on the principles of selflessness and compassion I'd learned as a Christian in my parents' home; sitting on a cushion practicing Zen; and studying the teachings of the Lakota Sioux." (Explicitly triangulates Christianity, Zen Buddhism, and Lakota spirituality as the three roots of his ego-dissolution method.)
- On ego suppression: "In Buddhism, suppressing the ego is central to any kind of awakening." His coaching is described as "denying individual egos for the benefit of the team."
- On arguing/faith: "Arguing isn't where faith is. That just feeds the ego."
- On zazen/meditation's purpose: "The point of Zen practice is to make you aware of the thoughts that run your life as they arise and diminish their power over you."
- On the trap of NBA fame: "It's easy for players to get so caught up in the fantasy world of the N.B.A. that they lose touch with reality. My job, as I see it, is to wake them out of that dreamlike state and get them grounded in the real world."
- Core mantra, widely quoted: **"Selflessness is the soul of teamwork."** Elaboration: a two-count rule on holding the ball — "when you become the focus, our system breaks down." The most valuable players might average only 4–7 points but win through "ability to play in a selfless manner." Jackson explicitly preferred a roster of "more selfless and group-oriented" but less individually talented players over stacked but selfish ones.
- On surrender producing higher performance (a direct ego-dissolution-enables-flow claim): "When a player surrenders his self-interest for the greater good, his fullest gifts as an athlete are manifested. He's not trying to force a shot... by playing within his natural abilities, he activates a higher potential beyond his abilities, a higher potential for the team."
- On emergent collective intelligence: "A powerful group intelligence emerges that is greater than the coach's ideas or those of any individual on the team." Players become "totally connected to one another, in sync with one another, like five fingers on one hand."
- On vulnerability as the gateway to selflessness: teams must "check that attitude at the door — that defensiveness, that protection of your own image" — vulnerability "turns into strength."
- **Sources:** Tricycle Magazine review/interview (https://tricycle.org/magazine/sacred-hoops-spiritual-lessons-hardwood-warrior/ and https://tricycle.org/magazine/buddha-and-bulls/); Buddhistdoor Global (https://www.buddhistdoor.net/features/buddhism-and-basketball-phil-jackson-and-his-zen-coaching/).

### B. Tricycle Magazine — "Basketball and Buddhism: An Interview with Phil Jackson"
- On what stops young players from surrendering ego: "Ego. Selfishness." He notes players arrive having been "spoonfed" by entourages since adolescence, making it hard to "integrate them into a twelve-man unit and submit their will to the good of the whole."
- On "soft focus" as an anti-ego discipline: "Soft focus means that my way is not the only way; it's not letting the *I* get in the way of the *we*."
- On his own spiritual identity: describes himself as "Zen Christian" — meditation helped him move past a "hard fundamentalist" dualistic upbringing.
- On meditation instruction for players: "We try to give them the idea of being able to focus, by watching their breath and dealing with the thoughts that come into their minds."
- Uses gestalt language ("the whole is greater than the sum of the parts") and circle symbolism for unity.

### C. DailyGood — "The Soul of Teamwork," Phil Jackson interviewed by Ross Robertson
https://www.dailygood.org/story/430/the-soul-of-teamwork-phil-jackson-in-an-interview-by-ross-robertson/
- Reinforces the "selflessness is the soul of teamwork" framing with the same two-count/rhythm-destruction logic and the "five fingers on one hand" image.

### D. *Eleven Rings: The Soul of Success* (2013, with Hugh Delehanty)
- On the coach's own ego-management as a precondition for the team's: "dial back my ego and distribute power as widely as possible without surrendering final authority. Paradoxically, this approach strengthened my effectiveness because it freed me to focus on my job as keeper of the team's vision."
- On leadership and self-sacrifice: "As a leader your job is to do everything in your power to create the perfect conditions for success by benching your ego and inspiring your team to play the game the right way. But at some point, you need to let go and turn yourself over to the basketball gods." (Non-attachment to outcome, not just to self-image.)
- **On Michael Jordan:** Jackson got "the greatest player in the world" to "embrace selflessness, even if it meant" losses along the way. His summary of Jordan's duality: "The greatness of Michael Jordan is his competitive drive. The weakness of Michael Jordan is his competitive drive" — i.e., ego as simultaneously the source of genius and the thing that had to be redirected, not eliminated.
- **On Kobe Bryant / Michael Jordan meeting (an attempted ego-transference intervention):** Jackson "orchestrated a meeting between the two stars, thinking that Michael might help shift Kobe's attitude towards selfless teamwork. After they shook hands, the first words out of Kobe's mouth were 'You know I can kick your ass one on one'" — a small, telling anecdote about the limits of trying to teach ego-surrender by proximity to another ego.
- **On Kobe/Iverson (specific line used with Kobe on selflessness):** Jackson reportedly asked Kobe, "Do you see how Allen Iverson has to play?" — using Iverson's isolation-heavy, unaided style as a cautionary contrast to invite Kobe toward system basketball. (Basketball Network: https://www.basketballnetwork.net/old-school/phil-jackson-masterfully-handled-kobe-bryant-ego)
- **On Shaq/Kobe feud management:** Jackson read Shaq as the more emotionally reactive of the two and deliberately aligned closer to Shaq to stabilize the locker room, while Kobe (per Shaq's own later account, ESPN/Shaq Fu Radio/Yahoo Sports coverage) felt Jackson used press comments about him to appease Shaq — Kobe's read: "This MF just mindf***** me." Kobe later described Jackson turning their friction into fuel: Jackson would ask himself "how can I use that?" — i.e., channeling unresolved ego conflict productively rather than suppressing it outright. Results: three straight titles, 2000–2002.

### E. Triangle offense as structural ego-dissolution mechanism
- The offense has no primary ball-handler and no single decision-maker; all five players must read the defense continuously. Analysts describe it as removing the *option* of individual dominance: "Kobe couldn't iso-ball his way to a championship, Shaq couldn't just demand the ball. They had to trust each other or lose." This is the clearest case where Jackson embedded ego-dissolution directly into basketball *system design*, not just locker-room psychology — the structure forces selflessness rather than merely requesting it.
- Sources: PRWeek, CBS Sports, Adobe Blog, Hoop Mentality, Medium (Petra Ivanigova "The Zen of Phil Jackson").

### F. Meditation, mindfulness, and ritual practice with teams
- Jackson taught an abbreviated Zen-derived meditation practice to the Bulls, encouraging even 5–10 minutes before/after practice; the guiding phrase was "one breath, one mind." Reported practices: sitting in silence together pregame, deliberately shaping posture ("where their shoulders had to be... so they're not slouched"), dimming lights during film session, and — per multiple secondary sources — burning sage in the locker room.
- Jackson's own account (from Rick Rubin's Tetragrammaton podcast transcript, 2023 — technically outside the 12-month window but his most extensive on-record philosophical conversation, so flagged as essential background): "we just sit in quiet space... There was no need to talk. Just be quiet and sit and get centered." On team unity as transcendent: "the unity of a team playing well together is a spiritual event... It hooks you on sports when you're a kid," adding it resists articulation — "it's impossible, I have to say, you can't make a film about this... In fact, my teacher would say, it's before the moment."
- **The line-crossing ritual (a literal ego-surrender rite):** Jackson describes opening team sessions by lining players up and having them "step across the line as a physical way of expressing your obedience or your following" — a deliberate embodied ritual of submitting individual will to the collective enterprise before a season begins.
- **The jazz-quintet metaphor for distributed ego:** "basketball's kind of like a jazz quintet... I riffs off another guy and someone gets to play a little bit of a lead," with a cautionary contrast — "Coltrane, he wouldn't put that ax down. He had to play 40 minutes, sometimes all by himself. We don't wanna have that happen" — i.e., even genius-level individual expression must yield floor time to the ensemble.
- On competitive detachment from officiating/outcome ("no mind"): when facing a bad call, "no mind. That's not part of what you're doing" — a direct transplant of Zen "no-mind" (mushin) into in-game emotional regulation.
- Personal daily practice, continuous from playing days to present: "To this day, I sit in the morning and meditate."
- Sources: Basketball Network ("One breath, one mind" — https://www.basketballnetwork.net/old-school/phil-jackson-on-how-mindfulness-and-presence-became-the-bulls-secret-weapon-for-greatness); Podscripts.co transcript of Tetragrammaton episode; HuffPost/Oprah Super Soul Sunday video features; The Emotion Machine "10 Lessons in Zen Leadership."

### G. Dennis Rodman — managing an extreme individualist without erasing him
- Jackson's approach to Rodman was notably *not* full ego-suppression but bounded tolerance: "He believes in his own individual nature. He likes to have freedom and the expression that he has. We think that's O.K. as long as it stays within the team confines of playing ball together." This nuances the "death of ego" theme — Jackson's real target wasn't eccentricity or self-expression per se, but *selfishness that breaks team function*; Rodman was allowed a flamboyant public identity as long as his on-court behavior served the collective.
- Source: The Spokesman-Review, Oct 13, 1995, "Bulls Can Absorb Rodman's Ego Without Problem" (https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1995/oct/13/bulls-can-absorb-rodmans-ego-without-problem/); Basketball Network.

### H. Coaching philosophy synthesis (undated/recurring, "Coaching Coaches" Substack, Dec 15, 2024 — just outside the 12-month window but close and thematically core)
- "As a coach, your job is to connect with the human spirit of each player, help them trust you, and guide them to trust each other."
- "In coaching, less power is often more powerful. When you give players space, they step up and take ownership of the team."
- "The more I tried to exert power directly, the less powerful I became. Leadership is about distributing power widely while retaining final authority."
- "To lead effectively, you have to be calm, receptive, and truly in touch with yourself. Leadership is about setting the tone, not dictating it."

## Evolution and tensions worth noting for the book

1. **From suppression to redirection.** Early Sacred Hoops-era language leans toward "suppressing" or "denying" individual ego. By Eleven Rings and later interviews, the framing softens into *channeling* — Jordan's competitive "sickness" isn't eliminated, it's aimed; Rodman's individualism is bounded, not erased. The mature position looks less like ego-annihilation and more like ego-in-service-of-something-larger.

2. **The coach's own ego is part of the system.** A recurring, explicit claim (Eleven Rings, UW leadership piece, Tricycle) is that Jackson's *own* ego-dialing-back — distributing power, de-personalizing feedback, staying in the background — is the precondition that makes player ego-surrender possible at all. Carmelo Anthony's 2025 public rebuttal is the sharpest recent test of this claim: Anthony accuses Jackson of failing his own standard by staying "in the stands" rather than coaching the system himself.

3. **Disagreement with conventional star-centric sports culture.** Jackson positions himself explicitly against the modern NBA's isolation/three-point/superstar-showcase culture (All-Star Game remarks, Aug 2025; NPR interview, Nov 2025) — a direct, recent, on-record critique of an individual-over-system philosophy that dominates contemporary basketball, and by extension much of star-driven leadership thinking generally.

4. **Ego-dissolution is structural, not just motivational.** Unlike much leadership/sports-psychology writing that treats ego-management as an interpersonal or emotional skill, Jackson repeatedly builds it into *systems* (the triangle offense's no-primary-ball-handler design, the two-count rule, the line-crossing ritual, meditation as literal shared physiology via synchronized breath) — mechanism over exhortation.
